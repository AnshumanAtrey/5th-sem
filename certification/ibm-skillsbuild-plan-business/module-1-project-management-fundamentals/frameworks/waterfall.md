Waterfall

Think of a waterfall. The water flows downward rapidly, and once it moves downstream, it can’t go back upstream. The Waterfall framework is named after this natural flow because of its resemblance to the sequential movement.

Waterfall is a traditional and sequential project management framework where the project teams complete tasks in several phases that follow a linear order. The teams fully complete each phase before moving to the next phase, making it a structured and predictable approach.

PMs and project teams often use the Waterfall framework for projects with fixed requirements, scope, and budget, ensuring minimal changes during project execution. For such projects, the phases in the Waterfall framework help teams follow a clear and organized approach to a project.

To understand what these phases are and how PMs and project teams navigate them, consider an example.

Rafi is managing a project to create a new board game for families. He recognizes that each step, from defining the game rules to manufacturing the final product, must be completed in sequence to avoid costly changes. If the team starts production before finalizing the rules or artwork, they might need to reprint game boards or remake pieces, leading to wasted materials and time. To reduce the risk of rework, Rafi decides to adopt the Waterfall framework that can help his team complete each phase sequentially.

The Waterfall framework helps teams complete each phase thoroughly before moving forward, creating a step-by-step path to project completion. For PMs, this framework is a reliable approach that is ideal for projects with fixed requirements and minimal expected changes.

As you explore Waterfall and other frameworks, remember that the tasks that PMs and project teams complete during each phase might vary depending on the industry and the specific nature of the project. For some projects, such as the board game development, it might involve creating physical layouts, and for other projects, such as the cybersecurity initiative, it might involve designing risk assessment strategies, security protocols, and response plans. Regardless of the industry, the phases remain applicable, even if the specific tasks differ.
