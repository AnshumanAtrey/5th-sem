Look at the databefore trusting it

Exploring the data (EDA, exploratory data analysis) means counting and drawing it before building anything, the way you'd read logs before writing detection rules. It shows what separates attacks from normal traffic and catches problems early. We explored a fair sample of 4,58,995 rows (step 3 shows how we picked it).

How the families differ

Pick a number. For each family, the dark tick is the middle row, the shaded box holds the middle half of the rows, and the thin line covers 90% of them. The axis grows ×10 at every step, so tiny and huge values both fit.

Normal traffic (bytes): the middle row is 248; half the rows are between 100 and 1,054; 90% between 66 and 1,945. Point at a family to read its numbers.

Middle row, biggest to smallest: ■ Mirai botnet 573 · ■ Spoofing 334 · ■ Normal traffic 248 · ■ Web attacks 143 · ■ Brute force 120 · ■ Recon 108 · ■ DDoS 54 · ■ DoS 54

What we learnedFloods (DDoS, DoS) are not faster on average. What gives them away is tiny packets (the middle DDoS row is 54 bytes) and connections that last a split second. Recon, web and brute force sit inside normal traffic's range on almost every number: those will be the hard ones to catch.

Columns that say the same thing

Correlation is a score from 0 to 1 for how strongly two columns move together. 1 means one column is just a copy of the other.

Point at a square: darker means the two columns move together more.

What we learnedRate and Srate are identical, and so are IPv and LLC. Copies don't hurt most models, but naive bayes treats every column as separate evidence, so it counts the same clue twice. That is one reason it does badly in step 5.

Squeezing 45 numbers onto a flat map

Each row is 45 numbers, so it's a point in 45 dimensions, which nobody can draw. PCA (principal component analysis) finds the 2 directions in which the rows differ most and lays every row out on them, like the shadow of a 3-D object on a wall: detail is lost, but the shape shows. Click a family to hide or show it.

Each dot is one real row. These 2 directions keep 36% of how rows differ; it takes 20 directions to keep 95%. So a flat picture is a rough sketch, which is why the models get all 45 numbers.

What we learnedMirai and the floods form their own tight clouds, so they should be easy. Normal traffic, recon, spoofing, web and brute force pile up in one shared cloud: the models will need all 45 numbers, and a lot of care, to pull them apart.

Which attacks look alike

We take each of the 34 labels' average row, join the two most alike labels, then the next most alike, and keep going until everything is one tree. This is hierarchical clustering. Joins near the left mean very alike.

Point at a label to light up its whole family.

What we learnedEvery DoS flood sits right next to its DDoS twin (the same attack from one machine or from many), and normal traffic sits right next to ARP and DNS spoofing. That predicts exactly what the models will struggle with in step 5.

The column that cheats

IAT is supposed to mean 'time since the previous packet'. But its values sit in neat bands near 0, 83 million and 167 million, and each family lives in its own bands. Real packet gaps don't look like that.

A decision tree naming all 34 labels, given…

IAT alone (1 column)87.7%
traffic features without IAT (45)74.9%
everything (46)90.2%

What we learnedIAT alone names the right label 87.7% of the time, more than all 45 real traffic numbers together (74.9%). It tracks when each attack was recorded in the lab, not the traffic itself. On a real network a new attack happens at a new time, so this clue would vanish. We threw IAT out before training anything.
