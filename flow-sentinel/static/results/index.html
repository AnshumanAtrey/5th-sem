Grade every modelon the locked exam

Each of the 11 trained models answers the 1,14,749 exam rows it has never seen (84,614 attacks and 30,135 normal). We compare every answer with the truth and count. Nothing here is a model's own opinion of itself: it is all counted on the exam.

Words used on this page

Exam rows
The 1,14,749 rows locked away in step 3. No model saw them while learning, so they show how it does on new traffic.
Confusion matrix
A 2×2 table sorting every exam answer: caught, missed, false alarm, or correctly left alone.
Recall (catch rate)
Of the real attacks, the share the model caught. The most important score for an intrusion detector.
Missed attack (false negative)
A real attack the model called normal. The dangerous mistake: the attacker gets in.
False alarm (false positive)
Normal traffic the model called an attack. Wastes the security team's time.
Precision
Of the alarms raised, the share that were real attacks.
Accuracy
Of all exam rows, the share answered right. Misleading here, because most exam rows are attacks.
Alarm threshold
The chance of attack at which we call it an attack. Default 50%; moving it trades catches for false alarms.

Every score comes from those 4 boxes

  • Recall (catch rate)

    Of the real attacks, how many were caught?

    81,184 ÷ 84,614

    95.95%

  • Precision

    Of all the alarms, how many were real attacks?

    81,184 ÷ (81,184 + 2,568)

    96.93%

  • False alarm rate

    Of the normal rows, how many were wrongly flagged?

    2,568 ÷ 30,135

    8.52%

  • Accuracy

    Of all the exam rows, how many were right?

    (27,567 + 81,184) ÷ 1,14,749

    94.77%

  • F1

    One score that drops if either precision or recall is bad

    2 × 0.969 × 0.959 ÷ (0.969 + 0.959)

    96.44%

Where clustering fits

Clustering means grouping similar rows without being told the answers (unsupervised learning, syllabus module VII). We used it twice, for two different jobs, and neither is the main detector.

1 · K-Means, as a detector

Learned 20 groups of normal traffic only (never saw an attack) and flags anything far from all of them. Score: catches 57.7% of attacks with very few false alarms (293).

By family: Mirai 94%, DoS 81%, DDoS 74%, but recon 23%, spoofing 7%, web 7%.

Why not the main detector: loud floods stand out from normal traffic, quiet attacks don't, so it misses most of the hard ones. Its value is that it needs no attack examples, so it could still flag a brand-new flood nobody has labelled yet.

2 · Hierarchical clustering, to understand the data

Built the family tree of the 34 labels in step 2 (Explore): it joins the most alike labels first.

It showed every DoS sitting next to its DDoS twin, and normal traffic sitting next to ARP and DNS spoofing.

Why it matters: it predicted exactly where the models would struggle: spoofing hides in normal traffic, and DoS and DDoS get mixed up when naming the family. It explains results; it doesn't make predictions.

(PCA, the 2-D map in step 2, is not clustering: it squeezes 45 columns into 2 for a picture, it doesn't group rows.)

Is it luck?

One exam could flatter a model by chance. 5-fold cross-validation repeats the test 5 times: the training rows are cut into 5 parts, and each time the model learns from 4 and is graded on the 5th.

Attacks it has never seen

New attacks appear all the time. So we retrained gradient boosting 7 times, each time with one whole family removed, then tested it on that family. Next to it, the K-Means detector, which never saw any attack at all.

What we learnedA new kind of flood is still caught (they look like other floods). A new quiet attack mostly isn't: spoofing drops to 46% when the model has never seen it.

The case study's six questions

Can machine learning tell normal and malicious traffic apart?
Yes. The model on this site catches 95.95% of attacks in the exam, and 96.93% of its alarms are real.
Which network features matter most?
rst_count, urg_count, Number, flow_duration, Header_Length: scrambling them hurts the model most (the 'hang up' and 'urgent' counts, batch size, duration and header size).
Which algorithm has the highest recall?
Bagging, 96.35% (tied with random forest), missing 3,086 of 84,614 exam attacks.
Which attack categories are hardest?
Recon and spoofing. Missed most often: Recon-OSScan (33%), DNS_Spoofing (21%), Recon-PingSweep (17%), MITM-ArpSpoofing (15%). They send a few ordinary-looking packets, not a flood.
How does class imbalance affect the model?
Accuracy misleads (always saying 'attack' scores 73.7%); naive bayes reaches 99.9% precision while catching only 27.9%. Weighting the rare families raises their catch rate (brute force 47% → 71%) at the big families' cost, without improving the total.
Can it detect previously unseen traffic?
Partly. Retrained without a family, it still catches 100% of Mirai but only 46% of Spoofing.

Limitations, said plainly

  • 8.5% of normal traffic is flagged at the 50% line. Moving the line to 90% cuts that below 1%, but more attacks get through.
  • All the traffic comes from one lab network; another network needs re-testing.
  • Each row summarises 10 or 100 packets, so the detector never sees single packets.
  • Our sampling set the mix of normal and attack rows, not a real network.
  • The IAT leak shows how easily a dataset can flatter a model; other columns could hide subtler leaks.
  • One layer of defence, not a guarantee.
