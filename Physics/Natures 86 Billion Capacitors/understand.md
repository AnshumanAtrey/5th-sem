# Understand: 86 Billion Capacitors

**How to use this:** read each **Say** out loud (about 40 seconds per slide). The **Q&A** under it is what the examiner is likely to ask.

**The one idea to keep repeating:** *each neuron's skin is a capacitor. Ion "batteries" charge it, "switches" fire it, and a leaky "wire" carries the signal.*

**Prefixes:** milli (m) = thousandth · micro (µ) = millionth · nano (n) = billionth · pico (p) = trillionth · femto (f) = 10⁻¹⁵ · kilo (k) = thousand · mega (M) = million

**Units:**
- V = volt (electric push)
- A = ampere (current)
- C = coulomb (charge)
- F = farad (capacitance)
- Ω = ohm (resistance)
- S = siemens (conductance)
- W = watt (power)
- J = joule (energy)
- Hz = hertz (times per second)
- T = tesla (magnet strength)
- K = kelvin (°C + 273)
- eV = electron-volt (the energy of one particle)

**Same letter, different meaning:** C = capacitance / coulomb / concentration · E = electric field / battery voltage / energy · m = milli / metre / mass

## Slide 1: 86 Billion Capacitors

**Say:** "Your brain has about 86 billion neurons. Each one is wrapped in a thin skin that works like a capacitor, so your brain is 86 billion capacitors charging and discharging. That is what a thought physically is. Right now there's an electric field of about 10 million volts per metre across every neuron in your head. I'll show that every part of a neuron is a circuit part from our syllabus: a capacitor, a battery, a switch, a wire and insulation."

- **What is a neuron?** A brain cell that carries electrical signals. Its parts:
  - dendrites (branches that receive signals)
  - cell body (the main part, which holds the nucleus)
  - axon (the long "wire" that sends the signal)
  - synapse (the hand-off point to the next cell)
- **Why "86 billion capacitors"?** The brain has about 86 billion neurons (counted in 2009), and each neuron's skin stores charge like a capacitor.
- **Ions leak through it, so is it really a capacitor?** Yes, a leaky one: a capacitor with a resistor side by side (in parallel). That's the RC circuit from our syllabus.
- **Where is the charge? Is the whole cell negative?** No. Only a very thin layer of extra ions sits on each face of the membrane (− inside, + outside), the way charge sits on capacitor plates. The rest of the cell is neutral.
- **Is the brain chemical or electrical?** Electrochemical. Chemistry (the difference in ions inside and outside) charges the battery, and physics (electricity) carries the signal.
- **Neuron vs nerve?** A neuron is one cell. A nerve is a cable made of many neurons' axons bundled together.

## Slide 2: The Frog, the Fight and the Battery

**Say:** "In 1791 Galvani saw a dead frog's leg kick when it was touched with metal. He said animals make their own electricity, and he pictured each muscle fibre as a Leyden jar, the first capacitor. So the idea of the body as a capacitor is over 230 years old. Volta disagreed: he said the two metals make the electricity. Trying to prove it, he built the first battery in 1800 and called it an 'artificial electric organ', modelled on the electric torpedo fish. In the end, both of them were right."

- **Why did the leg kick?** Two different metals plus salty frog tissue make a mini battery, like a lemon battery. Its current pushed the nerve past its firing point, the nerve fired, and the muscle contracted.
- **Where did the kick's energy come from?** From the muscle's own stored energy, ATP (adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy coin). The metal only pulled the trigger, the way a light switch doesn't power the bulb.
- **Why does a dead frog's leg still work?** Its cells stay alive and charged for a while after the frog dies, until their energy runs out.
- **What is a Leyden jar?** The first capacitor (1745–46): a glass jar with metal foil on the inside and the outside. The glass insulates the two charged foils from each other.
- **Battery vs capacitor?** A battery *makes* voltage from a chemical reaction and can push current for a long time. A capacitor makes nothing: it stores charge that something else put there and releases it quickly. In a neuron, the ion difference is the battery and the membrane is the capacitor.
- **How did Volta's pile work?** He stacked zinc and copper discs with salt-soaked cloth between them. Each pair makes a small voltage, and stacking them in series adds the voltages together.
- **Who was right?** Both. Volta was right that two metals in salty water make a battery. Galvani was right that animals make their own electricity; in 1794 he made a leg kick with no metal at all.

## Slide 3: Part 1, The Capacitor

**Say:** "The neuron's skin, the membrane, is a layer of fat only 5 nanometres thick, which is 16,000 times thinner than a hair. Fat doesn't conduct electricity, so it's the insulator. The salty water on each side does conduct, so those are the plates. Two conductors with an insulator between them make a capacitor. It holds minus 70 millivolts. That's tiny, but across such a thin gap it's 14 million volts per metre, five times what makes air spark. And Q equals C V shows that one spike moves only about 6,000 ions per square micrometre, so the cell barely notices."

- **What is a capacitor, and what is capacitance?** A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator (the physics word is *dielectric*). It stores + charge on one side and − on the other; a camera flash uses one. **Capacitance** is how much charge it stores for each volt, like the size of a bucket. Its unit is the farad (1 F = 1 coulomb per volt). That's a huge amount, so real values are given in µF. The membrane has about 1 µF per cm².
- **Explain C = εA/d.**
  - **C** = Capacitance
  - **A** = Area of the plates
  - **d** = distance (the gap) between the plates
  - **ε** (epsilon) = permittivity of the insulator, which equals K × ε₀
    - **ε₀** (epsilon-nought) = the permittivity of empty space, 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m
    - **K** = dielectric constant, about 2–3 for fat. The insulator's molecules line up and partly cancel the field, so more charge fits per volt.
  - Bigger plates or a thinner gap store more charge. The membrane is super thin, so its capacitance is big.
- **That's a flat-plate formula, but a cell is round. Why can you use it?** The membrane (5 nm) is thousands of times thinner than the cell (about 10–20 µm across), so each small patch is practically flat, the way the ground looks flat on a round Earth.
- **Calculate it.** C/A = Kε₀/d. Use d ≈ 3–4 nm, because only the oily middle insulates (the full 5 nm includes the water-loving heads on each surface).
  - Low end: (2 × 8.85×10⁻¹²) ÷ (4×10⁻⁹) ≈ 0.44 µF/cm²
  - High end: (3 × 8.85×10⁻¹²) ÷ (3×10⁻⁹) ≈ 0.89 µF/cm²
  - (1 F/m² = 100 µF/cm².) The measured value is about 0.9–1, so the estimate works.
- **Name each variable in Q = CV.**
  - **Q** = charge stored (coulombs)
  - **C** = capacitance (farads)
  - **V** = voltage (volts)
  - In words: charge = bucket size × push.
  - For one spike, the voltage swings about 0.1 V (from −70 up to about +30/+40 mV). So Q = 10⁻⁶ F/cm² × 0.1 V = 10⁻⁷ coulombs per cm².
  - One ion carries the elementary charge, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, so that's 6×10¹¹ ions/cm². Since 1 cm² = 10⁸ µm², it comes to **about 6,000 ions per µm²**. That's the minimum; real spikes use a bit more.
- **How did you get 10⁷ V/m?** E = V/d. **E** = electric field (push per metre), **V** = voltage, **d** = thickness. 0.07 V ÷ 5×10⁻⁹ m = 1.4×10⁷ V/m. Using the 3–4 nm core gives about 2×10⁷ V/m instead, which is the same ballpark.
- **Why doesn't the membrane spark?** Air sparks through an "avalanche": electrons speed up across the gap and knock more electrons loose, and a 5 nm gap is far too short for that. Fat can also take a stronger field. The membrane only tears open (called *electroporation*) at roughly 0.2–1 V across it, several times its normal voltage.
- **What does −70 mV mean?** The inside is 70 thousandths of a volt more negative than the outside, about 1/20 of a AA battery.

## Slide 4: Part 2, The Battery

**Say:** "A capacitor needs a battery to charge it. The neuron's battery is a difference in ions, which are charged atoms. There's 28 times more potassium inside the cell, and about 12 times more sodium outside. Potassium leaks out through tiny doors called ion channels and carries positive charge with it, so the inside turns negative. That continues until the electric pull balances the leaking, and the Nernst equation gives that balance voltage. The sodium–potassium pump keeps this battery charged using ATP. The electric eel stacks about 6,000 battery cells in series to reach 860 volts."

- **What are K⁺ and Na⁺?** Ions, which are atoms carrying an electric charge.
  - **K⁺** = potassium ion (K comes from the Latin *kalium*). Bananas are rich in it.
  - **Na⁺** = sodium ion (Na comes from the Latin *natrium*). Table salt is sodium chloride.
  - The **+** means the atom lost one electron, so it carries one positive charge.
- **What are the "doors"?** Ion channels: tiny protein tunnels through the membrane, each letting only one kind of ion through. Leak channels are always a little open. Voltage-gated channels snap open when the voltage changes; they are the switches on Slide 5.
- **What is mM?** Millimolar: thousandths of a mole per litre. A mole is just a counting word for 6×10²³ particles, the way a "dozen" means 12. So mM tells you how crowded an ion is.
- **Explain the Nernst equation: V = (RT/zF) · ln(C_out/C_in).**
  - **V** = the balance voltage for one type of ion
  - **R** = gas constant (8.314 joules per mole per kelvin)
  - **T** = temperature in kelvin (body: 37 °C = 310 K)
  - **z** = the ion's charge (+1 for K⁺ and Na⁺)
  - **F** = Faraday constant (96,485 coulombs per mole, the charge of one mole of electrons)
  - **ln** = natural logarithm (log to base e ≈ 2.718)
  - **C_out, C_in** = concentration outside and inside (*here C means concentration, not capacitance*)
  - In words: the voltage at which the electric pull exactly cancels the ions' urge to spread out.
- **Why is temperature in it?** Heat makes ions jiggle and spread from crowded places to empty ones (diffusion), and RT is that heat energy per mole. The balance is reached when the electric energy (zFV) equals the urge to spread (RT × ln of the ratio). That's the thermodynamics.
- **Show the calculation.** RT/F = (8.314 × 310) ÷ 96,485 ≈ 26.7 mV. Changing ln to log₁₀ multiplies by 2.303, which gives **61.5 mV for every 10× difference**.
  - K⁺: 61.5 × log₁₀(5/140) = 61.5 × (−1.45) ≈ **−89 mV ≈ −90 mV**. Negative means the inside is negative.
  - Na⁺: 61.5 × log₁₀(145/12) ≈ **+66 mV** (+60 to +70, depending on the Na⁺ inside).
- **Why is the resting voltage −70 mV and not −90 mV?** At rest, mostly K⁺ doors are open, which pulls the voltage toward −90. A little Na⁺ also leaks in and pulls it back up, so it settles near −70. The exact value comes from the Goldman equation, which is a Nernst equation for all the ions at once, each weighted by how open its doors are.
- **What does the Na⁺/K⁺ pump do? Does it make the −70?** It throws 3 Na⁺ out and brings 2 K⁺ in for every 1 ATP it uses. It's the *charger* that keeps the ion difference topped up. The −70 itself comes mainly from K⁺ leaking out; the pump adds only a few millivolts directly.
- **Series vs parallel (the eel vs the ray)?** The eel's cells are electrocytes, modified muscle cells.
  - **Series:** voltages add. The eel gets 6,000 × 0.14 V ≈ 860 V.
  - **Parallel:** currents add and the voltage stays low. The ray gets about 50 V but more than 1 kW.
  - Why the difference: freshwater resists current, so the eel needs high voltage. Seawater conducts well, so the ray needs high current.

## Slide 5: Part 3, The Switch

**Say:** "Now the capacitor sits charged at minus 70 millivolts. If something pushes it up to about minus 55, the threshold, sodium doors snap open. Sodium rushes in and the voltage flips to about plus 40. Then the sodium doors lock shut, potassium doors open, potassium rushes out, and the voltage resets. All of this takes 1–2 milliseconds, and the spike is always full size: all-or-none. In 1952 Hodgkin and Huxley wrote this down as a circuit made of a capacitor, batteries and variable resistors. Their equation predicted the signal speed within about 10%: 18.8 metres per second predicted, 21.2 measured. They won the Nobel Prize in 1963."

- **What is an action potential?** The neuron's electrical spike. The voltage jumps from −70 mV to about +40 mV and back in 1–2 ms.
- **What is the threshold, and why is the spike all-or-none, physically?** The threshold (about −55 mV) is the minimum voltage needed to fire. Past it, **positive feedback** takes over: a little Na⁺ comes in, the voltage rises, more Na⁺ doors open, more Na⁺ comes in, and so on. So it always runs all the way to the top, like a toilet flush.
- **Why does Na⁺ rush in?** Two forces push it in: there's more Na⁺ outside, and the negative inside attracts positive ions.
- **Why +40 and not +60?** The top is capped by the sodium battery (E_Na ≈ +60 to +70 mV), not by how hard the neuron was pushed. The spike peaks near +40 because the Na⁺ doors lock and the K⁺ doors open just before it gets there.
- **Name the variables: C_m·dV/dt = −g_Na(V−E_Na) − g_K(V−E_K) − g_L(V−E_L) + I**
  - **C_m** = membrane capacitance
  - **V** = membrane voltage
  - **dV/dt** = how fast V is changing
  - **g_Na, g_K, g_L** = conductance: how open the sodium, potassium and leak doors are (= 1/resistance, measured in siemens)
  - **E_Na, E_K, E_L** = the battery voltage for each door. E_Na and E_K come from the Nernst equation; E_L is a measured value for a mix of ions. *Here E means a battery voltage, not a field or an energy.*
  - **(V − E)** = driving force: how hard that ion is being pushed
  - **I** = current injected from outside
  - **In words:** capacitor current = injected current − the current leaking out through the doors. The minus signs are there because current flowing *out* of the cell is counted as positive, and outward current lowers V.
- **Where does C·dV/dt come from?** Current is charge per second (I = dQ/dt). Differentiating Q = CV gives I = C·dV/dt. Each door then adds Ohm's law: I = g × (V − E).
- **What makes the resistors variable?** Gates that open and close with voltage. Hodgkin and Huxley wrote g_Na = ḡ_Na·m³h and g_K = ḡ_K·n⁴. Here ḡ = maximum conductance, m = sodium opening gate, h = sodium locking gate and n = potassium gate.
- **What is patch clamp, and what is a pA?** Patch clamp is a glass micro-straw sealed onto the membrane that records the current through **one single channel**. pA = picoampere = 10⁻¹² A, a trillion times less than a phone charger's current. Even so, 1 pA ÷ 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C ≈ 6 million ions per second, so a 1–2 pA channel passes about 10⁷ ions per second.

## Slide 6: Part 4, The Wire

**Say:** "The spike has to travel down the axon, the neuron's long wire. But it's a leaky wire: salty water inside, and every patch of membrane is a capacitor that must charge before the signal moves on. William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, worked out this same maths in 1854 for undersea telegraph cables. That's why bare nerves crawl at 0.5 to 2 metres per second. Nature's fix is myelin, a fatty insulation. It makes d bigger, so C gets smaller: less charge to fill and less leak. Now the signal jumps from gap to gap at up to 120 metres per second. In multiple sclerosis, this insulation gets damaged."

- **Why is a nerve slower than copper wire?** In copper, the signal is an electromagnetic wave travelling at about 2/3 the speed of light. In an axon, no ion travels the whole length. Instead, each patch of membrane capacitor has to be charged through the salty core, which resists current tens of millions of times more than copper, before it fires and charges the next patch. It's a wave that rebuilds itself at every step, like a burning fuse, not an EM wave.
- **What is τ = RC, and why 63%?**
  - **τ** (tau) = time constant
  - **R** = resistance (ohms)
  - **C** = capacitance (farads)
  - A capacitor charges as V = V₀(1 − e^(−t/RC)), where V₀ = the final voltage and e ≈ 2.718. At t = RC this gives 1 − 1/e ≈ **63%** full.
  - A bigger R or C means slower charging. A resting neuron's τ is about 10–30 ms.
- **If τ is 10–30 ms, how is a spike only 1–2 ms?** During a spike the Na⁺ doors fly open and R drops about 100×, so the membrane charges about 100× faster.
- **The slide says "delay grows with length²". What does that mean?** That rule is for a **passive** cable with no boosting, like Kelvin's telegraph cable. A longer cable has more R and more C, so RC grows as length × length. A neuron re-fires the spike at every patch, like repeater stations along a cable, so the spike moves at a steady speed: double the length and the delay only doubles. The cable's RC sets how fast each hop is.
- **What is myelin, and why does it make the signal faster?** A fatty wrap made by support cells. Its many layers make d bigger, so C gets smaller (C = εA/d) and each hop needs less charge. The spike only re-fires at the bare gaps (the **nodes of Ranvier**). This is called **saltatory conduction**, from the Latin *saltare*, "to jump".
- **But myelin raises R, and τ = RC says a bigger R is slower?** There are two different resistances. Myelin raises the *wall* (leak) resistance, so current stops leaking out and reaches the next gap. The resistance *along the inside* doesn't change, and C is smaller, so each hop charges faster.
- **What is MS?** Multiple sclerosis. The immune system attacks myelin, so signals slow down or stop.
- **What do those speeds feel like?** 0.5–2 m/s is walking pace. 120 m/s is about 430 km/h, faster than a Formula 1 car.

## Slide 7: Part 5, The Code

**Say:** "Every spike is the same size, so how does the brain tell a feather from a hot stove? By how often the neuron fires: more spikes per second means a stronger signal. That's FM, not AM. Where two neurons meet, at the synapse, the electrical signal turns into a chemical messenger, crosses the gap, and becomes electrical again. That's the only real chemical step, and it takes half a millisecond to a millisecond. Some synapses skip the chemistry completely: gap junctions pass current straight through."

- **How does the brain code how strong something is?** By the firing rate (spikes per second), not by the size of each spike. This is called *rate coding* (Adrian, 1926).
- **FM vs AM?**
  - **FM** = Frequency Modulation: the information is in how often the wave repeats.
  - **AM** = Amplitude Modulation: the information is in how tall the wave is.
  - Neurons work like FM. Strictly, it's *pulse-frequency modulation*: the pulses are identical and the information is in how many arrive per second.
- **Why would nature use FM instead of AM?** Noise on a long, leaky wire mostly changes a signal's height, not its timing, so counting spikes survives it. That's the same reason FM radio crackles less than AM. Each spike is also rebuilt full size anyway, so its height can't carry information.
- **What limits the maximum firing rate?** The refractory period. After a spike the Na⁺ doors stay locked for about 1 ms, so f = 1/T = 1 ÷ 0.001 s = 1,000 per second at most (f = frequency, T = time per cycle). Most neurons stay at a few hundred. The refractory period also stops the spike running backwards, so it only travels one way.
- **What is a synapse?** The hand-off point between two neurons. The spike arrives, a **neurotransmitter** (chemical messenger) is released, it crosses a tiny gap, it opens doors on the next neuron, and a new electrical signal starts.
- **What is a gap junction?** A direct tunnel between two cells that lets current flow straight through. It's an electrical synapse and works almost instantly.

## Slide 8: The Scale

**Say:** "Now put it all together: 86 billion capacitors. Surprisingly, 69 billion of them are in the cerebellum, the small part at the back of the brain that controls balance, even though it's only 10% of the brain's weight. The cortex has 16 billion. There are about 150 trillion synapses in the neocortex alone, and 150,000 to 180,000 kilometres of insulated wiring at age 20, enough to go four times around the Earth. All of this runs on about 20 watts, the same as an iPhone charger. One NVIDIA H100 AI chip uses 700 watts: 35 times more."

- **How were the 86 billion counted?** Azevedo and colleagues (2009) dissolved brain tissue into an even "soup" of cell nuclei. They stained the neuron nuclei, counted them in a small sample, and scaled up.
- **Why does the small cerebellum hold 80% of the neurons?** Its main neurons (granule cells) are tiny and packed extremely tightly.
- **What is the neocortex?** The wrinkled outer layer of the brain, used for thinking, language and the senses.
- **What does 20 W mean?** A watt is 1 joule of energy per second. 20 W all day comes to 0.48 kWh (kilowatt-hour, one "unit" on your electricity bill). That's about 400 kcal (the calories on food labels), roughly 4 rotis.
- **A capacitor only stores U = ½CV². So why does the brain need 20 W?** U (stored energy) = ½ × 10⁻⁶ F × (0.07 V)² ≈ 2.5 billionths of a joule per cm², which is tiny. The real cost is that every spike lets ions flow through the door "resistors", where the energy is lost as heat, and the pumps then burn ATP pushing those ions back, in 86 billion neurons. The brain is 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body's energy.
- **Is comparing the brain with an H100 fair?** Only for how much power each one draws. They compute in completely different ways, so I'm not saying the brain is 35× better, just that it uses 35× less power than one chip.

## Slide 9: Reading the Capacitors

**Say:** "If the brain is 86 billion capacitors charging and discharging, we can listen in from outside, and every tool comes from a different chapter of physics. EEG measures tiny voltages on the scalp: that's electrostatics. MEG measures the brain's magnetic field, which is up to a billion times weaker than Earth's, using superconducting sensors. fMRI uses the quantum spin of protons in a strong magnet. And PET uses antimatter and E equals m c squared."

- **Full forms:**
  - **EEG** = Electroencephalography (*electro* + *encephalo* = brain + *graphy* = recording)
  - **MEG** = Magnetoencephalography
  - **fMRI** = functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging
  - **PET** = Positron Emission Tomography
  - **SQUID** = Superconducting QUantum Interference Device
  - **BOLD** = Blood-Oxygen-Level-Dependent
  - **FDG** = fluorodeoxyglucose
- **How does EEG work, and why is it only microvolts when a neuron has 70 mV?** Metal discs on the scalp pick up 10–100 µV. The skull and scalp weaken and smear the signal, so you only pick up anything when millions of lined-up neurons are active *together*; random activity cancels out. What adds up is mostly their slower synaptic currents, not the 1 ms spikes. Alpha waves (8–13 Hz) appear when you close your eyes.
- **Why does the brain make a magnetic field, and why are SQUIDs needed?** Any moving charge (current) makes a magnetic field. The brain's field is 10 fT–1 pT, which is 10⁸–10⁹ times weaker than Earth's. SQUIDs are superconducting loops (zero electrical resistance, which only happens when they're very cold, so they're cooled with liquid helium to −269 °C) that sense tiny changes in magnetic field. Newer OPMs (optically pumped magnetometers, which use lasers and atoms) can do it without helium.
- **Explain f = (γ/2π) · B (MRI).** Protons (the hydrogen nuclei in your body's water) behave like tiny spinning magnets. In a magnetic field they wobble like a tilted spinning top, which is called *precession*.
  - **f** = Larmor frequency: how many times per second the protons wobble
  - **B** = magnetic field strength (tesla)
  - **γ** (gamma) = gyromagnetic ratio, a fixed property of the proton (2.675×10⁸ radians per second per tesla). Dividing by 2π turns radians into full turns: γ/2π = 42.58 MHz per tesla.
  - At 3 T: 42.58 × 3 ≈ **128 MHz**. That's radio waves, just above the FM radio band.
- **How does fMRI see a thought?** Haemoglobin carries oxygen in the blood. Without its oxygen (deoxyhaemoglobin) it's **paramagnetic** (weakly magnetic) and dims the signal; with oxygen it's **diamagnetic** (almost non-magnetic). Active areas use more oxygen, but blood flow overshoots and brings in even more fresh blood, so there's less used-up blood and the area looks brighter. It's **indirect**: it sees blood, not neurons.
- **Explain PET and E = mc².**
  - **How PET works:** radioactive sugar (FDG: glucose tagged with fluorine-18) is injected, and busy neurons take up more of it. Inside the fluorine-18 nucleus a proton turns into a neutron and throws out a **positron** (an antimatter electron, with + charge). When the positron meets an electron, both vanish into two gamma rays of 511 keV.
  - **The letters:** **E** = energy, **m** = mass, **c** = speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s).
  - **Why 511 keV?** E = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg × (3×10⁸)² ≈ 8.2×10⁻¹⁴ J. Divide by 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ J per eV to get about 511,000 eV = **511 keV**. (1 eV is the energy one electron gains across 1 volt.)
  - **Why do the gamma rays go in opposite directions?** The pair is almost at rest, so their total momentum is about 0. Two gamma rays flying apart back-to-back keep it at 0 (conservation of momentum). That's why PET uses a ring of detectors that looks for pairs hitting at the same instant.

## Slide 10: Writing Back

**Say:** "Physics can also write into the capacitors. TMS uses Faraday's law: a fast magnetic pulse induces current in the brain and fires neurons, like a wireless phone charger. Focused ultrasound times 1,024 emitters so their waves meet at one millimetre-sized spot, treating tremor without opening the skull. Cochlear implants stimulate the hearing nerve, and about a million have been fitted. Brain–computer interfaces read spikes: a cursor moved by thought in 2006, speech decoded at up to 78 words a minute in 2023, and Neuralink's first patient in 2024. So: a thought is charge moving across 86 billion capacitors, and physics has learned to read it."

- **Full forms:**
  - **TMS** = Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (*transcranial* = through the skull)
  - **BCI** = Brain–Computer Interface
  - **FDA** = US Food and Drug Administration
  - **CT** = Computed Tomography (a 3-D X-ray scan)
- **Explain Faraday's law: EMF = −dΦ/dt.**
  - **EMF** = electromotive force (the induced voltage)
  - **Φ** (phi) = magnetic flux (the field × the area it passes through)
  - **dΦ/dt** = how fast the flux changes
  - **The minus sign** = Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change
  - For a coil with N turns: EMF = −N·dΦ/dt.
  - **In TMS:** 1.5 T in 0.2 ms means dB/dt ≈ 1.5 ÷ 0.0002 = 7,500 tesla per second. That induces an electric field of about 100 V/m in the brain, which drives current through the salty tissue and pushes neurons past threshold.
- **MRI uses 3 T and TMS only 1.5 T. Why does only TMS make neurons fire?** Faraday's law needs a *changing* field. MRI's main magnet is steady (dΦ/dt ≈ 0, so no induced voltage), while TMS jumps to 1.5 T in 0.2 ms. MRI's smaller switching "gradient" coils are deliberately kept slow enough not to twitch nerves.
- **How does focused ultrasound work?** Waves add up (**constructive interference**) when crest meets crest, which happens when their paths differ by a whole number of wavelengths: path difference = nλ (n = a whole number, λ = lambda, the wavelength). A CT scan shows how the skull slows sound at each point, so each emitter fires slightly early or late and all the crests hit one spot together. That spot heats up and is destroyed, while MRI watches the temperature live.
- **How does a BCI read thoughts?** Tiny electrodes in the brain record spikes from many neurons, and a computer learns which pattern means which movement or word.
- **How does a cochlear implant work?** A microphone picks up sound, a processor turns it into electric pulses, and electrodes inside the inner ear (the cochlea) stimulate the hearing nerve directly.

## Curveball questions

- **Is the brain quantum? Is consciousness quantum?** That's unproven, and it's not what I'm presenting. Tegmark (2000) calculated that quantum states in the brain would break down in about 10⁻¹³ s, far faster than neurons fire (about 10⁻³ s).
- **Where is the syllabus physics in this?**
  - Capacitors (C = εA/d, Q = CV, ½CV²)
